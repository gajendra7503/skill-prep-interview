A **hallucination** is output that sounds confident and fluent but is not
true: an invented circular number, a policy clause that does not exist, a
case citation with a plausible name and no reality behind it. You cannot
eliminate hallucinations completely, but you can make them much rarer and
much easier to catch.

## Why models fabricate

A language model generates the most plausible continuation of the text so
far. Plausible and true usually overlap, but not always. Fabrication is most
likely when:

- **The answer is not in the prompt** and the model's training knowledge is
  thin, outdated, or never existed (internal policies, recent regulations).
- **The prompt implies an answer must exist.** "What does Section 14.3 say
  about bonus payouts?" pressures the model to produce *something*, even if
  there is no Section 14.3.
- **Specifics are requested:** exact numbers, dates, names, statute
  sections, and citations are where invented details hide.

## Give explicit permission to say "I don't know"

Models are trained to be helpful, so make it clear that admitting
uncertainty *is* the helpful answer.

Weak:

```text
What is the maximum reimbursement for mobile bills under our policy?
```

Improved:

```text
<policy>
...
</policy>

Using only the policy above, what is the maximum monthly reimbursement for
mobile bills? If the policy does not state this, reply "The policy does not
specify this" and do not estimate a figure. A missing answer is far less
costly to us than a wrong one.
```

The final sentence explains the stakes, which helps the model weigh
abstaining against guessing.

## Ground answers in provided sources

Wherever accuracy matters, give the model the source text rather than
relying on its memory, and restrict it to that text. For internal
documents this is the only option, since the model never saw them in
training. For external rules — tax slabs, regulator circulars — paste the
current official text, because the model's knowledge has a cutoff date and
may be out of date.

## Ask for citations to the supplied text

Require every factual claim to point back to the source, and make
unsupported claims something the model must remove.

```text
Answer the question using the documents provided. After each claim, cite
the document index and quote the exact sentence it is based on, in the
form [doc 2: "..."]. After drafting, check each claim: if you cannot find
a supporting quote, delete the claim and note that it was removed.
```

Citations to *supplied* text are checkable. Citations the model produces
from memory — URLs, report titles, case names — are not, and must be
treated as unverified.

## Build in verification steps

Ask the model to review its own work as a separate step, or run a second
prompt that checks the first:

```text
Below is a draft answer and the source documents. For each factual
statement in the draft, label it SUPPORTED (with the quote), NOT FOUND,
or CONTRADICTED (with the conflicting quote). Output a table.
```

Self-checking catches many errors but not all, so for high-stakes content
pair it with human review.

## Never trust generated specifics without checking

Treat these as unverified until a person checks them against the original
source:

| Type | Example risk |
| --- | --- |
| Numbers and calculations | A total of ₹4.2 crore that should be ₹42 lakh |
| Statutes and sections | A section number that has been renumbered or never existed |
| Case citations | A real-sounding judgment that was never delivered |
| Regulator circulars | A circular number and date that do not match any issued circular |
| Quotes | Wording "quoted" from a document that is actually paraphrased |

For arithmetic, ask the model to show its working or, better, compute the
figures in a spreadsheet or code. For legal and regulatory references,
always confirm against the official source before anything is sent to a
customer, regulator, or court.

### Try it yourself

1. Ask a question about a pasted document that the document does *not*
   answer, first without and then with explicit permission to say "I don't
   know". Compare the replies.
2. Add a citation requirement with exact quotes to one of your document
   prompts, then verify three of the quotes by hand.
3. Write a checker prompt that labels each claim in a draft as SUPPORTED,
   NOT FOUND, or CONTRADICTED.
