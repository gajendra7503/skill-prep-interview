You have probably heard colleagues say "just ask the AI" or "put it through
ChatGPT or Claude". This lesson is for you if you have never used one of
these tools, or have tried one a couple of times without really knowing what
was going on. No coding, maths or technical background is needed. By the end
you will have a simple mental model and the vocabulary to follow the rest of
the course.

## What generative AI actually is

Most software you use at work follows fixed rules written by a programmer:
an Excel formula always gives the same answer, a payroll system always
applies the same tax slab. **Generative AI** is different. Instead of
following hand-written rules, it has learned patterns from an enormous
amount of text, and it uses those patterns to **generate** new text — an
email draft, a summary, a list of ideas, an explanation.

The tools you will use are called **AI assistants** or **chat assistants**.
You type a request in ordinary language, and the assistant types a reply.
Claude (made by Anthropic) is the assistant used in this course's examples,
but the ideas apply to every similar tool.

A useful way to picture it: an AI assistant is like a very well-read new
colleague who joined today. They write quickly and know a little about
almost everything, but they know nothing about *your* company, *your*
customers or *your* deadline unless you tell them. They also sometimes
sound sure of themselves when they are wrong.

## The core vocabulary

You will meet these words throughout the course. You do not need to
memorise them now; come back to this table whenever a term is unfamiliar.

| Term | Plain-English meaning |
| --- | --- |
| Model | The trained "brain" behind the assistant, for example Claude Opus or Claude Sonnet |
| LLM | Large language model: the type of model that works with text |
| Prompt | Whatever you type or attach to ask the model for something |
| Response (or output) | What the model writes back |
| Conversation (or chat) | A back-and-forth thread of prompts and responses |
| Context | Everything the model can see right now: your messages, attached files, earlier replies |
| Token | A small chunk of text (roughly three to four English characters) that models read and are billed in |
| Hallucination | A confident-sounding answer that is false or made up |
| Training cutoff | The date after which the model learned nothing new on its own |
| API | A way for software, rather than a person, to send prompts to a model |

## What you can use it for at work

Here are tasks where AI assistants already save professionals hours every
week:

- **Writing**: first drafts of emails, circulars, meeting invites and
  proposals.
- **Summarising**: turning a 20-page policy or a long email thread into five
  bullet points.
- **Rewriting**: making a message more polite, shorter, simpler, or suitable
  for a customer instead of a colleague.
- **Explaining**: "Explain what a service-level agreement is, as if I am new
  to procurement."
- **Brainstorming**: ten possible names for an internal newsletter, or
  questions to ask in an interview.
- **Spreadsheets and data**: suggesting an Excel formula, or explaining what
  an existing one does.

## What it is not good at

Knowing the limits is what separates confident users from disappointed ones.

- **It can be wrong while sounding right.** Always check facts, figures,
  names and dates before you use them.
- **It does not know your company's private information** — your products,
  prices, clients or internal policies — unless you paste the relevant text
  in, and only if your company allows that.
- **It may not know recent events**, because of its training cutoff, unless
  the tool has web search switched on.
- **It is not a calculator.** For exact sums, use Excel or a calculator, or
  ask the assistant to write a formula you can check.
- **It cannot take responsibility.** If you send it, you own it.

## A first example

Here is the kind of request a new user might type, and a typical reply. The
company, "Kaveri Home Loans", is fictional.

```text
Prompt:
Write a short, polite email to our team at Kaveri Home Loans reminding them
that expense claims for October must be submitted by 5 November.

Response (shortened):
Subject: Reminder: October expense claims due 5 November

Hi team,
A quick reminder to submit your October expense claims by Wednesday,
5 November. Please attach all receipts so Finance can process them on time.
Thank you!
```

Notice two things. First, the model wrote something useful from a single
sentence. Second, it **made up a detail**: it guessed that 5 November is a
Wednesday. That may or may not be true for the year you are in, which is
exactly the kind of small error you must check before sending.

## Rules of the road

Before you type anything into an AI tool at work:

1. **Use only tools your company has approved.** Free public tools may store
   or learn from what you type.
2. **Never paste confidential data** — customer names, account numbers, PAN
   or Aadhaar details, salaries, passwords — unless your company's policy
   explicitly allows it for that tool.
3. **Read before you send.** Treat every AI draft as a first draft written by
   a capable but brand-new colleague.

The lesson on responsible AI later in the course covers these rules in depth.

### Try it yourself

Without using any AI tool yet, write down three tasks from your own last
week that involved drafting, summarising or rewriting text. For each one,
note what information an assistant would need from you to do it well (for
example: who the reader is, what the deadline is, what tone to use). Keep
this list — you will use it in the next lesson when you have your first
conversation with an assistant.
