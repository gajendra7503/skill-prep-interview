Modern models follow instructions **literally and precisely**. That is a
strength when your instructions are clear and a liability when they are
vague, contradictory or exaggerated. This lesson covers five habits that make
prompts behave predictably.

## Vague vs specific

Vague words like "short", "professional", "detailed" or "improve" mean
different things to different people. The model has to guess, and its guess
may not match yours.

```text
Summarise this report. Keep it short.
```

```text
Summarise this quarterly operations report for the regional head, who has
two minutes before a meeting. Use exactly five bullet points, each under 20
words. Lead with the single most important change since last quarter.
Include figures in lakh or crore as they appear in the report.
```

Specific prompts replace adjectives with **measurable targets**: a word
count, a number of items, a named reader, a concrete ordering rule.

A quick checklist for specificity:

- Could two people read this instruction and produce very different outputs?
- Is every adjective ("brief", "formal") backed by something concrete?
- Have I said what to do with edge cases, such as missing data?

## Explain the why

An instruction with a reason generalises better than a bare rule. When the
model understands the purpose, it can apply the spirit of the rule to cases
you did not anticipate.

```text
Never use abbreviations.
```

```text
Avoid abbreviations such as "KYC" or "TAT" unless you spell them out first,
because this FAQ will be read by first-time customers who do not know our
internal terms.
```

The second version tells the model that the real goal is readability for
newcomers. It will now also avoid jargon you did not list, and it knows that
spelling out a term first is acceptable.

## Say what to do, not only what not to do

Negative instructions ("don't use bullet points", "don't be wordy") tell the
model what to avoid but leave it guessing about what you want instead. They
can also draw attention to the very thing you are trying to avoid.

| Negative only | Positive and specific |
| --- | --- |
| Don't use markdown. | Write in plain flowing paragraphs suitable for pasting into an email. |
| Don't be too technical. | Explain as you would to a branch manager with no IT background. |
| Don't make it long. | Keep it under 120 words. |

You can keep a negative instruction when it matters, but pair it with the
behaviour you want.

## Avoid contradictions

Long prompts written over several edits often contain instructions that
pull against each other, and the model has to choose which one to obey.

```text
Be concise. Cover every clause of the leave policy in detail.
Use a friendly, casual tone. Write formally for the HR committee.
```

When you find a conflict, decide which goal wins and say so, or explain how
both apply:

```text
Write for the HR committee in a formal tone. Cover only the three clauses
that changed in the 2026 leave policy revision, in two to three sentences
each. If an unchanged clause is needed for context, mention it in one line.
```

Re-read long system prompts specifically looking for conflicts. They are the
most common cause of "inconsistent" behaviour.

## Calm wording beats shouting

Older models sometimes ignored instructions, so prompt writers learned to
shout:

```text
CRITICAL!!! YOU MUST ALWAYS RESPOND IN JSON. NEVER EVER ADD ANY OTHER TEXT.
THIS IS EXTREMELY IMPORTANT!!!
```

Current models, including Claude, pay close attention to instructions
already. Aggressive emphasis tends to cause **over-application**: the model
treats the shouted rule as more important than everything else and applies
it rigidly, even where it no longer makes sense. For example, a shouted "NEVER
mention competitors" can make a model refuse to discuss a customer's
comparison question at all.

A calm, explained version works better:

```text
Respond with a single JSON object and no surrounding text, because your
output is parsed directly by our ticketing system and any extra characters
will cause the import to fail.
```

Use emphasis sparingly, and when one instruction genuinely matters more than
others, explain why rather than adding capital letters.

## Putting it together

Before you send a prompt, read it once with these questions in mind:

1. Is each instruction specific enough to check?
2. Does each important rule carry its reason?
3. Have I said what I want, not just what I do not want?
4. Do any two instructions conflict?
5. Is the tone calm and explanatory?

### Try it yourself

Find a prompt you or your team uses regularly, ideally one that sometimes
produces odd results. Mark every vague adjective, every bare rule without a
reason, every negative-only instruction, and every pair of conflicting
instructions. Rewrite it using the five habits, then run both versions on
the same three inputs and compare the consistency of the results.
