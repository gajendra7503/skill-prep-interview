Most chat interfaces and every LLM API let you send two kinds of text: a
**system prompt** that frames the whole conversation, and **user messages**
that carry each individual request. Knowing what belongs in each — and how
to write a role that actually changes behaviour — is the difference between
a generic assistant and one that sounds like it works at your company.

## What a system prompt is

The system prompt is text the model reads before any user turn. It sets the
standing context: who the assistant is, who it is talking to, what it must
always or never do, and what format it should default to. It persists across
every turn of the conversation, so it is the natural home for anything that
should not change from one request to the next.

In the Claude API it is the top-level `system` parameter, separate from the
`messages` list. In consumer chat apps the equivalent is a project
instruction or custom-instructions field.

## A role is more than a job title

"You are an expert" is the weakest possible role. It tells the model almost
nothing about *which* expertise, *for whom*, or *under what constraints*.
A useful role describes the situation the assistant is operating in.

Weak:

```text
You are an expert customer support agent.
```

Improved:

```text
You are the first-line support assistant for Kavach Home Insurance, a
fictional insurer used in our internal training portal. You reply to
policyholders who write in by email, mostly about claim status, document
requirements, and renewal dates.

Most customers are not familiar with insurance terms, and many write in a
mix of English and Hindi. They are often anxious because a claim is pending.

Your goal is to give a clear next step in every reply. You cannot approve,
reject, or promise claim amounts — only the claims team can — so when a
customer asks about money, explain the process and offer to escalate.
```

The improved version gives the model the company, the channel, the
audience, the emotional context, the goal, and the limits of its authority.
Each detail changes what a good reply looks like.

## Tone and audience

State the audience explicitly rather than describing a tone in the
abstract. "Friendly but professional" is vague; "write as a senior colleague
explaining to a new joinee in their first week" is concrete. Compare:

| Instruction | What the model has to guess |
| --- | --- |
| Be concise and professional | How short? Professional for whom? |
| Reply in 3-5 sentences for a branch manager who reads on mobile between meetings | Very little |

Explain *why* when a rule exists. Modern models follow instructions
literally and generalise from reasons. "Never use ALL CAPS IN REPLIES!!!"
works worse than "Avoid all-caps and exclamation marks, because customers
read them as shouting." Calm, explained instructions beat shouting at the
model in capitals.

## Persistent rules vs per-request instructions

Split your instructions by how often they change:

- **System prompt (persistent):** identity, audience, tone, safety and
  compliance limits, default output format, escalation rules.
- **User message (per request):** the specific document, the customer email
  being answered, the task for this turn, any one-off format change.

```text
[system]
You draft replies for the HR helpdesk of Sahyadri Logistics (fictional).
Employees ask about leave, payroll dates, and reimbursements. Base answers
only on the policy text supplied in each message. If the policy does not
cover the question, say so and suggest emailing hr-desk@sahyadri.example.

[user]
<policy>...leave policy text...</policy>
Employee question from Ritu Sharma: "Can I carry forward 12 earned leaves
into next year?"
```

If you find yourself pasting the same paragraph into every user message, it
belongs in the system prompt. If the system prompt keeps growing with
special cases for one task, that content probably belongs in the request.

## Keeping system prompts maintainable

System prompts in production behave like code: they get edited by several
people, and a careless change can break behaviour silently.

- **Use sections.** Group content under clear headings or XML tags such as
  `<role>`, `<audience>`, `<rules>`, and `<output_format>`, so a reviewer
  can find and change one area without rereading everything.
- **Remove contradictions.** "Always be brief" and "always explain your
  reasoning in detail" in the same prompt force the model to pick one.
- **Prefer principles to long lists of cases.** A rule with a reason
  covers situations you did not anticipate; twenty special cases do not.
- **Version it.** Keep the prompt in source control and re-run a small set
  of test inputs whenever it changes.
- **Do not hide secrets in it.** Users can often coax a model into
  revealing its system prompt, so never put passwords or API keys there.

### Try it yourself

1. Take a one-line role you have used ("You are a helpful assistant for
   sales"). Rewrite it to name the organisation, channel, audience,
   goal, and limits of authority.
2. List every instruction in an existing prompt and mark each as
   *persistent* or *per-request*. Move them to the right place.
3. Find one rule written in capitals or with "NEVER" and rewrite it as a
   calm sentence that includes the reason for the rule.
