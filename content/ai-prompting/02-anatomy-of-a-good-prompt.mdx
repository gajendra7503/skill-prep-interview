Most weak prompts fail for the same reason: the writer knows far more about
the task than they put on the page. The model is capable, but it cannot read
your mind, your inbox or your team's unwritten conventions. This lesson gives
you a checklist of six building blocks and a template you can reuse every day.

## The brilliant new colleague

Picture a highly capable colleague who joined this morning. They are smart,
fast and willing, but they know nothing about your company, your customers
or why this task matters. If you handed them your prompt as a sticky note,
could they do excellent work without coming back with questions?

This mental model fixes most problems instantly. You would never tell a new
joiner "write the email" and walk away. You would say who it is for, what
happened, what tone to use and what a good result looks like. Write prompts
the same way.

A useful test: if a colleague would need to ask a clarifying question, the
model probably needs that answer in the prompt too.

## The six building blocks

| Block | Question it answers | Example |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Task | What exactly should be produced? | "Draft a reply to this customer email" |
| Context | What background is needed? | "The customer's refund was delayed by our payment partner" |
| Audience | Who will read the output? | "A retired customer, not technical, writing in English" |
| Format | What shape should the output take? | "Under 150 words, plain text, no bullet points" |
| Constraints | What must it respect or avoid? | "Do not promise a date; our policy allows 7 working days" |
| Success criteria | How will we judge it? | "The customer should know what happened and what happens next" |

Not every prompt needs all six spelled out at length, but checking each one
takes ten seconds and catches most gaps.

## Weak vs improved

A weak prompt:

```text
Write a reply to this complaint.

[customer email]
```

The model will produce something generic: polite, vague and possibly
promising things your team cannot deliver.

An improved prompt that covers the six blocks:

```text
You are helping the customer support team at Nirmal Home Appliances, a
fictional consumer electronics retailer.

Task: Draft a reply to the customer email below.

Context: Mr Rajesh Iyer ordered a ₹42,000 refrigerator on 2 October. Delivery
was delayed because our logistics partner had a warehouse flood in Chennai.
The new delivery window is 15 to 18 October. We are offering free
installation (normally ₹1,500) as an apology.

Audience: The customer is frustrated and has written twice already. He wants
a clear answer, not corporate language.

Format: Plain text email, under 150 words, with a greeting and sign-off from
"Priya, Customer Care".

Constraints: Do not blame the customer or name the logistics partner. Do not
promise a specific day within the window, because the partner has not
confirmed it.

Success criteria: After reading, Mr Iyer should understand why the delay
happened, when to expect delivery, and what we are doing to make up for it.

<email>
[customer email]
</email>
```

The second prompt is longer, but every line removes a guess the model would
otherwise have to make.

## Context is the most skipped block

People usually remember the task and forget the context. Context includes:

- **Why** the task exists ("this summary goes to the regional head before a
  budget meeting").
- **What has already happened** ("we sent a reminder last week").
- **Relevant data** (the policy text, the numbers, the previous email).
- **Conventions** ("we write dates as 12 Oct 2026", "amounts in lakh").

Telling the model the purpose often improves output more than any wording
trick, because it can then make sensible decisions about details you did not
think to specify.

## A reusable template

Keep a template like this in a notes file and fill it in for any recurring
task:

```text
Task: [one sentence: what to produce]

Context: [background, purpose, what has happened, relevant facts]

Audience: [who reads it, what they know, what they care about]

Format: [length, structure, tone, file or text type]

Constraints: [what to respect or avoid, and why]

Success criteria: [what a great result achieves]

Input:
[the document, data or message to work on]
```

Once the template is filled in, read it as the new colleague would. Remove
anything irrelevant, and add anything they would still need to ask about.

## Iterate, do not restart

Your first prompt rarely gives the perfect answer. When the output misses,
diagnose which block was weak. Too long? Tighten the format. Wrong tone?
Describe the audience better. Made up a policy? Supply the policy text as
context. Changing one block at a time teaches you what actually matters.

### Try it yourself

Pick a task you did at work this week, such as summarising a meeting or
replying to a vendor. Write the lazy one-line prompt you would normally use,
then rewrite it using the six-block template. Run both and compare: which
blocks made the biggest difference, and which could you drop for this task?
