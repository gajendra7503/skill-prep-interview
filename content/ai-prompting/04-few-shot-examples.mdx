Describing what you want in words works well, but sometimes showing is
faster and more precise than telling. Examples are one of the most reliable
ways to control format, tone and edge-case handling. They are also easy to
misuse, because models copy examples very faithfully, including the parts
you did not mean them to copy.

## Zero-, one- and few-shot

| Style | What you give | Good for |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Zero-shot | Instructions only | Common tasks the model already does well |
| One-shot | Instructions plus one example | Showing a specific format or tone |
| Few-shot | Instructions plus several examples (typically 3 to 5) | Classification, extraction, consistent style, tricky edge cases |

Start with zero-shot and clear instructions. Add examples when the output
format drifts, when the tone is hard to describe, or when the model keeps
mishandling a particular kind of input.

## A few-shot classification prompt

Suppose a fictional bank's support team wants incoming emails tagged by
category.

```text
Classify each customer email into exactly one category: ACCOUNT_ACCESS,
CARD_ISSUE, LOAN_QUERY, COMPLAINT or OTHER. Respond with the category only.

<examples>
<example>
<input>I've been locked out of net banking after three wrong passwords.</input>
<output>ACCOUNT_ACCESS</output>
</example>
<example>
<input>My EMI for the home loan went up this month, why? Loan no. HL-20931.</input>
<output>LOAN_QUERY</output>
</example>
<example>
<input>Third time I'm writing. Nobody from the Andheri branch has called back.
This is unacceptable.</input>
<output>COMPLAINT</output>
</example>
<example>
<input>Can you send me a form for updating my address?</input>
<output>OTHER</output>
</example>
</examples>

<email>
[new email here]
</email>
```

Notice the structure: each example sits inside an `<example>` tag, and each
has a clearly labelled `<input>` and `<output>`. The wrapper `<examples>`
separates demonstrations from the real input in the `<email>` tag, so the
model never confuses an example with the thing it should classify.

## Choose diverse, representative examples

Good examples cover the **range** of inputs the model will actually see.

- **Vary length and style.** Include a one-line email and a rambling one, a
  polite one and an angry one, English and Hinglish if your customers write
  that way.
- **Cover every category** or output type at least once, especially the
  rare ones.
- **Include hard cases.** An angry email about a card being blocked is
  CARD_ISSUE, not COMPLAINT, if that is your rule. Show that boundary.
- **Use realistic data.** Examples copied from real (anonymised) traffic
  teach more than tidy invented ones.

## Over-fitting to surface features

Models pick up patterns you did not intend. If all your examples share a
surface feature, the model may treat that feature as part of the rule.

Common traps:

- Every example output starts with "Dear Sir/Madam", so every reply does,
  even to a customer who signed off with her first name.
- All positive-sentiment examples are short and all negative ones are long,
  so the model starts judging sentiment by length.
- Every example summary has exactly three bullets, so a document with seven
  important points still gets three.
- The examples all mention Mumbai, so the model inserts Mumbai where a city
  is missing.

Fixes:

1. Vary the incidental features across examples, so only the intended
   pattern is consistent.
2. Say explicitly which aspects to copy: "Match the tone and structure of
   these examples, but adapt the length and greeting to each customer."
3. Test with inputs that differ from all your examples and check for
   copying.

## Examples plus instructions, not instead of them

Examples work best alongside a clear description of the task. Instructions
explain the rule; examples show how the rule applies at the edges. If an
example contradicts your instructions, the model will often follow the
example, so check them for consistency.

A weak prompt that relies on a single example:

```text
Rewrite like this: "Your KYC is pending" -> "Please complete your KYC
verification to continue using your account."

Now rewrite: "Payment failed"
```

An improved version:

```text
Rewrite short system alerts into polite, complete sentences for customers.
Explain what happened and the next step, in under 25 words. Keep any
reference numbers exactly as given.

<example>
<input>KYC pending</input>
<output>Your KYC verification is incomplete. Please upload your documents
in the app to continue using your account.</output>
</example>
<example>
<input>Txn TX8812 failed - insufficient balance</input>
<output>Transaction TX8812 could not be completed due to insufficient
balance. Please add funds and try again.</output>
</example>

<input>Payment failed</input>
```

### Try it yourself

Write a few-shot prompt that extracts the customer name, policy number and
requested action from insurance service emails for a fictional insurer. Use
four examples wrapped in `<example>` tags with labelled input and output.
Deliberately make the examples vary in length, language mix and formality.
Then test with an email unlike any example, and check whether the model
copied anything it should not have.
